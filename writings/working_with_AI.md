# The Psychology of Working With AI in 2026

*It was Friday afternoon. I sent out a Claude to make an org chart of my team of ~40 Claude researchers.*

*But something about it felt off. This was not how I wanted work to be.*

I wrote down these lines back in April, trying to start a post on how AI has reshaped work and the mental challenges of adapting to that shift. But I never got to finish the piece. Things have been changing faster than my understanding and writing could catch up, and there are too many moving parts. But precisely because of how fast things have been changing, I find that this topic deserves timely discussion. So, instead of pulling together a coherent story as I usually try to, I'll just offer a collection of observations, commentaries, and arguments on scattered topics.

## We've seen this before, but it's too fast this time

I do not think the current "AI revolution" is that unique compared to humanity's past technological revolutions. Its impact, at the end of the day, will probably be similar to what came before — society goes on "normally", with people taking different jobs and some overall improvement in productivity and (hopefully) well-being. Even the rapid pace of AI's progress is not that out of place, since it just falls along the past trajectory of each industrial revolution (steam engine, electricity, computer, internet) taking less time than previous ones.

What is different is that the human timescale is more fixed, so "just faster" does have a lot of significance, now that the rate of technological progress is much faster than a typical human can absorb. ChatGPT was released in late 2022, less than 4 years ago. But now AIs are solving [Erdős problems](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-the-legendary-erdos-problems-are-falling-to-ai-20260803/) and [making progress on the Riemann Hypothesis](https://www.anthropic.com/research/riemann-zeta) and [breaking out of sandboxes](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-mythos-preview-system-card) and [collectively hacking major websites to get better scores on evals](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-model-evaluation-security-incident/). In fall 2025, when I just started my current job, Claude was just beginning to become a reasonably good coder and the best practice was "write nothing, review everything". Six months later I had an org chart of my Claudes and some of them don't even directly report to me. If you want to maximally harness frontier models' capability, my estimate is you need to change how you work every three months or so. That's basically being put in a new job every three months with no onboarding. This speed can cause a variety of issues. It leaves the stressful impression that choices made now have high stakes because they can compound during this rapid progress. It does not allow enough time for education to catch up, so people are given a complex tool with no proper training. It deepens the divide between work and life, as the two now proceed at very different paces.

## The adolescence of automation

Now (mid 2026) is an awkward moment in automation with AI.

Not long ago, AI was great at a few things (coding, casual chat) but significantly sub-human in most things, so it still felt like a more traditional tool that just automates away a few well-defined tasks. It was already mind-blowing, thinking that such a feat is even possible, but in practice it was like any other good tool, and adoption was pretty much pure joy and no effort.

In the very distant future (i.e., 2–3 years later, given my distorted sense of time), AI will be good enough that you can basically treat it as a capable person, and either stop working or choose to work on what you enjoy. Or die in poverty if the whole economic system fails to adapt. Either way "how I work" is not something we'd worry about on a day-to-day basis.

But it's hard right now. Now is a time when frontier models are good at nearly everything — but not literally everything. So the nature of working with AI feels less like AI taking away some of my work but more like AI taking away most of my work and dumping something very different on me. Altogether it's a huge productivity win for sure, but a large part of my work becomes something I don't enjoy as much — like context engineering, asking Claude to stop writing slop, and constantly jumping back and forth between multiple projects.

It is like transitioning from a postdoc to a faculty (or from an IC to a manager, for you tech people), with the exception that you have an infinite supply of capable but annoying students, and that the way they are annoying changes slightly every few months. Note that all of these differences are load-bearing. (Yes, I'm fluent in Claude-speak now.) The infinite supply of "intelligence" and the fact that AI is so good in so many areas give you an urge to keep doing more and pushing the boundary of how much you can automate. Yet once you have pushed far enough you begin to expose issues in the model, and fixing or adapting to those issues becomes your primary job. The fact that model capabilities improve and issues are being fixed actually makes this worse, as these improvements encourage you to push even further and become bothered by different problems every few months. One personal example on this: Claude was never a terrific writer, but it was not a problem when I only used it as a coder. But now, when most of my time is spent reading Claude's summary and interpretation of nuanced experiments, this causes a lot of (metaphorical and literal) headaches.

## Maximizing efficiency is no longer maximizing joy

Let me give a slightly more formalized argument on the point above. Consider the efficiency and joy you get at different levels of adoption of some technology (or level of automation). There are two main factors that affect both: the time freed by having the technology do your work for you, and the additional effort of using the technology to do what it cannot do on its own. For more traditional technology, a nice property is that it has a very clear scope of applicability and doing anything beyond this scope is basically impossible. Your calculator doesn't write emails for you. So both efficiency and joy begin to decrease beyond that scope, and the optimum is clear and aligned: you use the technology for what it can, and nothing more. With AI what gets tricky is that there is no longer a sharp boundary between "can" and "cannot". Instead, there is a whole spectrum in between. You can go a long way in holding its hand (or wiping its butt) a little more and gain more efficiency. But that could be at the cost of joy, as you are trading your normal work for something you might enjoy less. In summary, this grey area between can and cannot allows the maximum of efficiency to decouple from the maximum of joy.

Putting one's joy in tension with their productivity is nothing new. That's basically what happens when you decide whether you want to work overtime. But traditionally this tension lies in how hard or how much you work, not how you work, and those of us believing "work smart, not hard" are slow to realize that "work smart" is the new "work hard".

## What even is the meaning of work?

Currently, using AI to work still feels like an amplification of our productivity. You can do more work in the same amount of time, and the math gets better as the fraction of work done by AI increases; your productivity further increases, and you get more job security thanks to that productivity. But there is a catch: once we reach a point where AI can do all of your work, it is no longer an amplification but a pure replacement.

In some sense this has already happened. If someone has a specific piece of code to write in mind, and will need to choose between hiring me and using a frontier AI, a year or so ago I think they'd still choose me but now the AI would be the clearly better choice. I haven't felt this replacement yet only because my work covers a variety of tasks and AI is only replacing some of them, but I doubt that will remain true for long.

There might be, of course, futures where a small fraction of work is reserved for humans, for example things we need a human being to take responsibility for whatever the model outputs. But even with that it doesn't feel quite the same: if all I do is connect a plug in a data center, it's hard to say I have a strong connection to all the work the AI there produces.

So here's what I'm trying to say. Even if some of us still feel that AI makes them more productive and increases their economic value, it probably won't be long before virtually all of us become worthless in an economic sense. And the challenge is in accepting that and adapting to that.

There is, of course, an economic aspect of it: our society needs to find new ways to keep people alive and well (or in a darker future, decide that this is not necessary) despite them producing no value. But I don't want to dig into this as people have been talking about this often enough and this issue is already present in our current society where productivity is overly concentrated in a small fraction of people.

What I want to focus on is the psychological implications of how we find meaning in work. Right now there's not much problem in this: yes, many are underpaid due to capitalism, but most do believe their work is valuable. But it becomes very different when what you get to produce is simply not valuable anymore as AI (and robots controlled by AI) already produce more than enough of that.

As a species, humans have evolved a tendency to attach some significance to producing something valuable as this can help us survive. But this instinct can hurt once we are no longer capable of producing anything valuable. What do we do then? At a rational level, we can simply say we need to accept the fact that there won't be much meaning in work anymore and try to find meaning elsewhere, and stop weighing productivity so heavily as a life goal. But we should not ignore the fact that "finding meaning in work" is so deeply ingrained socially (and maybe even genetically) that we can't simply change our instincts. What we really need is to clearly conceptualize this disconnect between reality and our instincts and find ways to pacify them.

It sounds abstract, but it has happened before. The concept of physical exercise is a relatively new thing; it emerged only after, in some societies and communities, humans' physical labor became so unnecessary that the instinct of our body to move a lot (evolved for hunting and gathering) could not be satisfied. Again, the main difference is just the pace; the adoption of "exercise" as a concept took a few generations but we probably need some form of that to satisfy our urge to find meaning in work much sooner. In fact, we might already have something in place: some hobbies do just that.

## Beyond productivity: you can use AI to optimize (nearly) everything, but you don't have to

There are two extremes I often see these days. Some truly believe that AI can be used to optimize everything and spend a significant fraction of their life doing it. Others dislike that attitude and insist that there are things that AI simply cannot do and some aspects of work and life should remain as they are because they are uniquely human.

I think both are making a mistake in assuming a strong causal relation between "AI can optimize everything" and "I should use AI to optimize everything". In other words, the assumption that optimizing for productivity is a must.

And this can explain a lot of anxiety and resentment from both sides. Some, upon realizing that AI does have the potential to take part in pretty much everything, find themselves obliged to make this happen, creating a ton of anxiety from what they don't do and fatigue from what they actually do. Others, upon feeling the fatigue of having to use AI to optimize everything and (rightfully) rejecting this over-optimization, subconsciously try to justify this choice by claiming that AI can't help with everything.

But allowing that tension — that AI can optimize almost everything yet any single one of us should not be obliged to do that — is the key to both staying open-minded about new technology and respecting the limitations of humans and the notion that we have some meaning in life beyond productivity.

## On artisanal work, control, and feedback

Here is a case study of how one can go beyond productivity and optimize for the joy of work.

The type of "traditional" research that I used to do before the AI age has a particular artisanal nature, which makes it quite enjoyable. That artisanal nature, however, seems largely gone as I adopt AI more, and I'd like to understand why.

The sense of control could be one key aspect in the joy of artisanal work: you know what's going to happen, you can make variations the way you like. Even on things where the outcome is inherently random or where the environment could change (e.g., you want different amounts of fuel for ceramics in different weather), what you should do is known. You have a perfect sense of what should be done, and the rest is just execution based on some input (your mood, the weather, etc).

But that sense of control is missing once AI does most of my work. The outcome is quite unpredictable, with the AI often failing in surprising ways. Nor do I have the granular control I used to have; if I only have 15 minutes to instruct my Claude to write a report, it's impossible to make sure every detail is perfect. Overall that just creates a lot of situations where I can see the result not being fully satisfactory but I don't have an easy way to fix it.

Another key difference is the level of feedback you get. In traditional artisanal work there is a tight loop of feedback: you do something, observe the outcome, adjust, and iterate. The key is that you can try out many small perturbations to build a deep intuition of how something should be done.

With AI this is a lot harder. I can no longer build that kind of intuition by trying out perturbations, because each segment of work is so long that the variation in the outcome is often dominated by the AI's own randomness. Moreover, the feedback is getting sparser: I can propose an experiment, and its result will arrive two days later, after I have rotated between ten other projects; I simply don't retain enough context to meaningfully learn from such feedback. This reminds me of the time when I learned to make pour-over coffee; I only make a single cup a day, and by the next cup I already forget the details of the previous one and can't reliably compare the outcome. So even now I still don't know whether I have optimized my brew.

However, I don't think that control and feedback have to be out of reach once one uses AI extensively. The difference is just that these are no longer something that naturally comes with the work, but something one has to explicitly plan the work routine around.

Let me be more specific. I think the essence of control and feedback in artisanal work lies in two aspects: One, close observation of outcomes and the resulting intuition on how your actions map to outcomes. Two, knowing what to do even in the face of what you cannot fully control, instead of feeling at a loss. To do the first means that you want to intentionally increase the observability of the project. Being intentional is the key here: AI is capable enough that it can default to only showing you the final result (and, at least for now, often in a low-quality presentation), and anything more requires your explicit ask. So think about what you would need to get good observability, and have your AI follow that standard. For example, I could have a standard format of listing experiment setups and ablation tables, which includes details I find relevant but Claude might forget to list on its own. Or I could ask for a weekly compute report to understand how my Claude has been doing resource management. Generally the goal is that, for any important feedback I get when I work on my own, I can try to have my AI produce that for my review too. And to do the second means spending less effort coming up with ad-hoc solutions and thinking more about general protocols. When Claude is messaging me on five different projects, in what order should I read them? When I see a writeup from Claude, how do I provide feedback? When I notice a recurring error, how do I make sure it doesn't happen again? Over the past couple of months I have been evolving my work protocol to better answer questions like these, and it does significantly improve my well-being.

It is also worth noting that AI is on its own a very good tool for achieving better control and feedback. It can, for example, handle a lot of logistics and allow me to focus on the feedback that matters. It can also synthesize information that me alone would not be able to synthesize easily, like auditing all my past transcripts to find behavior patterns, or gathering experiment data into convenient dashboards. The key is using AI to have more of what you care about, not just to accelerate what the job demands.

## Enjoy the moment

We are in a turbulent time. Technology evolves so rapidly that it is hard to make reliable long-term projections on how one's current choices will impact their life in the long run. When everything is so uncertain, it no longer makes sense to plan extensively for a distant future. Instead, choosing what to do based on personal passion and on whether one would enjoy the process has become the more rational choice.

Moreover, this particular turbulent time might play an outsized role in our memory. Whether we end up in pure chaos or happily retire into abundance, I expect us to look back at now, at the late 2020s, as the time when a lot of stories happened. And I hope my memory of this time will be characterized by the fun of exploring new ways to work that suit me and new research areas that attract my interest, not the agony and fatigue of maximizing my productivity and impact.
